Creator’s Statement

Journeys of Knowledge

My project is a small interactive game that introduces players to the world of Renaissance technical knowledge through a choice-based structure. The game is intentionally simple. The player chooses a time period, selects a character from that era, makes four decisions, and then receives a short message about what their experience reveals. Even with such a scope, the game is built directly from the ideas in Pamela O. Long’s Chapter 6, “Openness and Authorship I: Mining, Metallurgy, and the Military Arts.” Her chapter helped me think about how to translate historical arguments into a quick and accessible experience.

Long explains that technical knowledge in the sixteenth century circulated among many different groups. Artisans developed skill through hands-on experience and often protected certain techniques as secrets. Scholars organized knowledge through books, translations, and treatises. Military engineers worked with practical mathematics and drew diagrams that helped guide decisions on fortifications and artillery. Court officials and patrons influenced which knowledge became public and which remained restricted. Although each group had different goals, all of them constantly had to decide whether to reveal information, hide it, or transform it through writing. Long’s discussions of authors like Biringuccio, Agricola, and Tartaglia show how complex these decisions could be and how strongly they were tied to ambition, politics, and practical needs.

In the game, the player chooses one character and then faces two short decisions that reflect the kinds of choices described in the chapter. The artisan decides whether to share a skill with an apprentice or protect it. The scholar chooses between relying on ancient texts or observing a workshop firsthand. The military engineer weighs the value of making knowledge public against the strategic danger of doing so during conflict. Each path ends with a short takeaway that summarizes what that character reveals about Renaissance technical culture.

My intended audience is students who have never thought about the history of science in terms of everyday labor, politics, or competing motivations. I want the game to show that technical knowledge did not simply progress on its own. It was shaped by social relationships, personal decisions, and the environments in which people worked. The game presents these ideas in a quick and approachable format. Even though it can be played in just a few minutes, it still encourages the player to consider how skill, authorship, and power shaped the early development of technical arts.

Works Cited

Long, Pamela O. “Openness and Authorship I: Mining, Metallurgy, and the Military Arts.” Openness, Secrecy, Authorship: Technical Arts and the Culture of Knowledge, Johns Hopkins University Press, 2001, pp. 175–210.
